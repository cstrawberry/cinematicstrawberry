Reflexive Undecidability: Computational Limits from Dynamic Interaction

Abstract

Classical undecidability comes from self-reference applied to a fixed input. A program is handed a description, and the description does not change while the program runs. Many real limits have a different shape: the act of finding out alters what is being found out. Measuring a quantum system changes its state. Evaluating an economic plan consumes the resources the plan allocates. Driving down a road changes the traffic on the roads next to it.

Reflexive Computational Systems formalize that shape. A system is a state space together with an interaction function that returns an outcome and a transformation function that consumes the outcome and returns the next state. The transformation reads the outcome as well as the interaction, and that dependence is what makes the structure reflexive. Deterministic and non-deterministic variants are given, and three consequences are proved: prior states can be unrecoverable, information obtained by interaction describes the state the interaction produced, and no prediction is stable when the system is built to falsify it.

Two undecidability results follow, and they are different results. Fixing a property in advance and reducing the halting problem to it shows that no interactive machine decides termination across a family of these systems, with the bound surviving bounded-error randomization. Letting the property depend on the predictor's own forecast shows something else: in a counter-predictive system, no interactive machine can finish with a recorded verification that matches the state it has produced. The first result is undecidability in the classical sense on an interactive family. The second is the failure of a fixed point, and it is what distinguishes reflexive limits from static ones.

Reflexivity in Plain Terms

Publish a poll a week before an election and the poll changes the vote. Nobody lied. People who see their side comfortably ahead stay home, and people who see it just behind turn out. The number was accurate when it was taken, and publishing it made the election different from the one it described.

That pattern has a shape worth naming. Something is done, a result comes back, and the result itself determines how the thing being examined changes. Not just the act, the result: a high reading leads somewhere different from a low one. Once the loop closes that way, three familiar troubles follow.

The first is that you may not be able to work backwards. Two different starting points can end at the same place after a measurement, so the reading plus the outcome do not tell you where you began. The second is that what you learn is already out of date, and not by accident: the reading describes the state your reading produced. The third is the sharpest. If the system is built to do the opposite of whatever you predict, then announcing an accurate prediction makes it wrong, and announcing an inaccurate one makes it right. There is no answer that stays true once given.

The last case is worth separating from the familiar one. Turing showed that no program can decide, for every program, whether it halts. There the question is fixed and the difficulty is self-reference in the encoding. Here the question is not fixed: it is about a system that reacts to being asked. Both limits are real, they have different sources, and a stubborn traffic jam that worsens wherever the navigation app sends people is an instance of the second kind.

1. Limits That Come from Interaction

Every discipline that studies its own limits has found a version of this. In physics, measurement disturbs what it measures, and the disturbance depends on the outcome obtained. In thermodynamics, recording and erasing information costs energy, a fact Landauer (1961) fixed at kBT ln 2 per erased bit. In computer science, Turing (1936) showed that a fixed question about programs can be undecidable, and Rice (1953) extended that to every non-trivial semantic property. In economics, publishing a forecast moves the market the forecast is about, which Grunberg and Modigliani (1954) and Simon (1954) analysed as a problem about fixed points.

These look like separate difficulties, and one structure is common to them. Information is not acquired from outside the system. The acquisition is an event inside it, and the event's outcome determines how the system changes. Reflexivity names that structure, and Reflexive Computational Systems make it precise enough to prove things about.

A concrete case makes the target clear. A traveller plans a route through a city where each road taken changes the traffic on the roads nearby, and the change depends on which road was taken. The planner cannot treat the map as fixed, because the map is a function of the plan being executed. This is not a harder version of an ordinary routing problem. It is a routing problem whose instance is defined by the solution attempt, and the same reversal appears in measurement, in planning, and in self-prediction.

2. Reflexive Computational Systems

2.1 The Deterministic Case

Definition 2.1 (D-RCS). A Deterministic Reflexive Computational System is a tuple S = (X, Y, O, V, T) where X is a non-empty set of states, Y a non-empty set of interactions, O a non-empty set of outcomes, V: X × Y → O the interaction function, and T: X × Y × O → X the transformation function. Applying y to x yields the outcome o = V(x, y) and leaves the system in state T(x, y, o).

The whole content is in the third argument of T. A transformation of the form T(x, y) would describe a system that is disturbed by being probed, which is ordinary. Writing T(x, y, o) says that the disturbance depends on what the probe returned, so two runs of the same interaction that happen to yield different outcomes leave the system in different places. That is the reflexive coupling, and everything below follows from it.

2.2 The Non-Deterministic Case

Definition 2.2 (ND-RCS). A Non-Deterministic Reflexive Computational System is a tuple S = (X, Y, O, Vprob, Tprob) with X, Y, O as above, Vprob: X × Y → Δ(O) assigning to each state and interaction a distribution over outcomes, written P(o | x, y), and Tprob: X × Y × O → Δ(X) assigning to each state, interaction, and realized outcome a distribution over next states, written P(x′ | x, y, o).

The coupling survives the move to distributions. The outcome is drawn first, and the distribution over next states is conditioned on the outcome that was drawn. A model in which the next state depended only on (x, y) would be an ordinary Markov decision process; the conditioning on o is what keeps it reflexive.

2.3 The Cycle

Both variants run the same loop. From state x an interaction y produces outcome o, deterministically through V or by sampling Vprob; the outcome then selects the next state through T or by sampling Tprob; and the loop repeats from x′. A run is the sequence

x₀ →(y₀, o₀) x₁ →(y₁, o₁) x₂ → …

where oi is the outcome at xi and xi+1 = T(xi, yi, oi).

2.4 Two Instances

A degrading sensor (deterministic). Let a state be a pair (temp, acc), the true temperature and the sensor's accuracy in [0,1], with the single interaction "measure". Let

V((temp, acc), measure) = ( temp·(1 + α(1 − acc)),  β(1 − acc) )

returning a reading biased by the accuracy shortfall together with an error estimate, and let

T((temp, acc), measure, (temp_m, err)) = ( temp − γ|temp − temp_m|,  max(0, acc(1 − δ)) )

so that the probe perturbs the temperature by an amount fixed by the reading it returned, and the sensor degrades. The transformation reads tempm, a component of the outcome, which is what makes the example reflexive rather than merely noisy.

Projective measurement (non-deterministic). Let X be the density operators on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, Y a set of observables, and O the union of their spectra. For an observable A = Σk λk Pk, let

P(λ_k | ρ, A) = Tr(P_k ρ)         (Born rule)
T_prob(ρ, A, λ_k) = δ at  ρ′_k = P_k ρ P_k / Tr(P_k ρ)   when Tr(P_k ρ) > 0

which is von Neumann's (1932) measurement postulate in the form Lüders (1951) gave it. The outcome is drawn from the Born distribution and the post-measurement state is determined by which outcome was drawn. Quantum measurement is a reflexive system in the exact sense of Definition 2.2, and the framework does not explain the Born rule, which enters here as an input.

2.5 What Makes This Different from Ordinary Computation

A Turing machine reads a fixed input. The machine's configuration changes as it runs and the input string does not, so a question about the input has an answer that stays put while the machine looks for it. Verification in complexity theory has the same character: an instance and a certificate are both static, and checking them changes neither.

In a reflexive system the object under examination evolves as a consequence of the examination, and the direction of that evolution depends on what the examination returned. This places the framework among models of interactive computation, where a machine exchanges values with an environment over time rather than consuming a fixed input. Wegner (1997) argued that interaction changes what computation means, and Goldin and colleagues (2004) gave the model used below: persistent Turing machines, which retain their configuration between interactions. Reflexivity adds one requirement to that setting, that the environment's transition depends on the outcome it returned, and the results below come from that requirement.

3. Three Consequences of the Cycle

Three results follow from the coupling alone, before any question of decidability arises. Each says what an observer interacting with such a system can and cannot come to know.

3.1 Prior States Can Be Unrecoverable

Lemma 3.1. (a) Let S be a D-RCS, fix (y, o), and let Xpre = {x ∈ X : V(x, y) = o}. If the map x ↦ T(x, y, o) is not injective on Xpre, then the post-interaction state together with (y, o) does not determine the pre-interaction state. (b) Let S be an ND-RCS. If for distinct x1, x2 the supports of Tprob(x1, y, o) and Tprob(x2, y, o) intersect, then observing a state in the intersection with (y, o) does not determine which preceded it.

Proof. (a) Non-injectivity gives distinct x1, x2 ∈ Xpre with T(x1, y, o) = T(x2, y, o) = x′. Both are consistent with the record (x′, y, o), so the record does not distinguish them. (b) Let x′ lie in both supports. Then P(x′ | x1, y, o) > 0 and P(x′ | x2, y, o) > 0, so both predecessors have positive likelihood given the record. ∎

The condition is a hypothesis about the system and not a theorem about all of them. A reflexive system whose transformations are injective loses nothing, which is why the condition appears explicitly wherever the loss is claimed.

3.2 Information Describes the State the Interaction Produced

Lemma 3.2. In a run x0, …, xn, the outcome on = V(xn, yn) is a function of xn and yn alone. In the non-deterministic case the outcome distribution Vprob(xn, yn) depends only on xn and yn. Any inference about x0 from on must therefore pass through the intervening transformations.

Proof. Immediate from the signatures of V and Vprob, which take the current state as their argument. The state sequence is generated by xi+1 = T(xi, yi, oi), so dependence on x0 survives only through the composition of those maps, and Lemma 3.1 gives conditions under which the composition is not invertible. ∎

This is the formal content of the blood-pressure cuff. Each reading is accurate about the state it found, and that state is one the reading helped bring about. Sequential measurements of quantities that do not commute have the same structure: the first measurement fixes the context in which the second is taken, so the pair of results describes a trajectory rather than a single prior state. The uncertainty relation is proved from the Hilbert-space representation and a supplied commutator, which is a separate argument, and the reflexive structure supplies the operational picture rather than the inequality.

3.3 No Prediction Is Stable Against a Counter-Predictive System

Definition 3.3 (Counter-predictive system). A D-RCS is counter-predictive when Y consists of predictions, O = {accurate, inaccurate} records whether the prediction holds of the current state, and the transformation inverts that status: T(x, y, accurate) is a state at which y is inaccurate, and T(x, y, inaccurate) is a state at which y is accurate.

Lemma 3.4. In a counter-predictive D-RCS, no prediction is accurate at the state its own registration produces. Formally, for every x ∈ X and y ∈ Y, the outcome o = V(x, y) and the state x′ = T(x, y, o) satisfy: y is accurate at x′ if and only if o = inaccurate.

Proof. Both cases are given by Definition 3.3. If o = accurate then x′ = T(x, y, accurate), at which y is inaccurate. If o = inaccurate then x′ = T(x, y, inaccurate), at which y is accurate. ∎

The registered outcome and the resulting state always disagree, so the pair (o, x′) is never consistent. This is the fixed-point failure at the centre of the framework, and Section 5 turns it into a statement about machines.

3.4 The Probabilistic Version

Softening the prediction into a probability does not restore stability. Let p(x, y) = P(accurate | x, y) be the chance that y holds at x, and suppose the transformation drives that chance the other way: for some δ1, δ2 > 0,

E[ p(x′, y) | x, y, accurate ]   ≤ p(x, y) − δ₁
E[ p(x′, y) | x, y, inaccurate ] ≥ p(x, y) + δ₂

Lemma 3.5. Under these conditions the endpoints are repelled. Writing p = p(x, y) and E[p′] = p·E[p′ | accurate] + (1 − p)·E[p′ | inaccurate], at p = 1 we get E[p′] ≤ 1 − δ1 < 1, and at p = 0 we get E[p′] ≥ δ2 > 0. Neither certainty nor certain failure is a fixed point of the accuracy dynamics.

Proof. Substitute p = 1 into the decomposition to get E[p′] = E[p′ | accurate], which the first condition bounds above by 1 − δ1. Substitute p = 0 to get E[p′] = E[p′ | inaccurate], which the second condition bounds below by δ2. ∎

Accuracy near one decays and accuracy near zero recovers, so a predictor facing such a system settles into an intermediate band. Perfect prediction and perfect anti-prediction are equally unavailable, which is the probabilistic image of Lemma 3.4.

3.5 What the Three Lemmas Give

  • Information loss, under the non-injectivity hypothesis of Lemma 3.1.
  • Context shift, unconditionally, by Lemma 3.2: what an interaction reveals is a property of the state that interaction produced.
  • Fixed-point failure, in counter-predictive systems, by Lemmas 3.4 and 3.5.

The first two constrain what can be learned; the third constrains what can be settled. Section 5 shows that the third is the one that yields a limit classical undecidability does not already cover.

4. Where the Structure Appears

4.1 Measurement

Section 2.4 already exhibits projective measurement as an ND-RCS. The observer effect is the dependence of Tprob on the outcome drawn from Vprob, and successive measurements of incompatible observables instantiate Lemma 3.2, since the second acts on the state the first produced. The framework supplies the operational shape of these facts. It does not derive the Born rule, the commutator, or the uncertainty relation, each of which requires the Hilbert-space structure as a separate input.

4.2 Irreversibility and the Cost of Records

Reversing a process to reach a specific earlier microstate requires knowing that microstate, and knowledge of it is obtained by interactions that form a reflexive cycle. Lemma 3.1 gives conditions under which those interactions destroy the distinctions needed, and Lemma 3.2 says the information obtained describes a later state. This is a constraint on the epistemic route to reversal, and it is not a proof that reversal is physically impossible: the second law is established by other arguments, and the reflexive account describes why an observer inside the process cannot assemble what reversal would require.

Where a transformation is logically irreversible, in the deterministic case a non-injective T and in the probabilistic case a Tprob that mixes distinct predecessors, its physical implementation obeys Landauer's (1961) bound of kBT ln 2 per erased bit, analysed further by Bennett (1982). A demon that sorts molecules must record outcomes and reset its memory, and those steps carry the cost, which is the reflexive reading of why the demon buys no free work. Whether a given RCS erases anything is a property of the specific T, so the thermodynamic consequence attaches to systems that are shown to be non-injective and not to reflexivity in general.

4.3 Economic Calculation

Mises (1920) and Hayek (1945) argued that a central planner lacks access to knowledge that only prices reveal. There is a second obstacle of a computational kind, and it is reflexive. Model the economy as a D-RCS whose state (R, P, U, C) records resources, production possibilities, utilities, and the planner's remaining computational budget. Let interactions be candidate plans, let V(x, A) = (feasible, metrics, CA) evaluate a plan and report the cost of evaluating it, and let T(x, A, o) deduct CA from the budget.

An optimal plan has to account for the cost of its own selection, and that cost is known only after the evaluation that consumes it. By Lemma 3.2 each evaluation reports on a state whose budget has already been reduced by earlier evaluations, so the planner never evaluates plans against the resources actually available at the moment of choosing. The knowledge problem and the computational problem are independent, and this model addresses the second.

4.4 Routing on a Map That Reacts

Definition 4.1 (D-RTSP). An instance is a triple (C, d0, τ) of cities, an initial distance function, and an update rule τ: D × E → D that revises distances after an edge is traversed. A tour π = (v1, …, vn, v1) has cost Σi di(vi, vi+1) where di+1 = τ(di, (vi, vi+1)). The non-deterministic variant replaces τ by τprob: D × E → Δ(D) and minimizes expected cost.

The mapping to Definition 2.1 is direct: states are distance functions, interactions are edges, outcomes are the costs incurred, and T(d, e, cost) = τ(d, e). A congestion rule that raises the cost of edges adjacent to the one just used gives the traffic example of Section 1.

Proposition 4.2. Let τ be computable in polynomial time and let all costs stay bounded by a polynomial in the instance size. Then deciding whether a D-RTSP instance admits a tour of cost at most B is NP-complete.

Proof. Membership: a tour is a certificate of size n, and verifying it requires n evaluations of τ and a sum of n costs, all in polynomial time. Hardness: take τ(d, e) = d, which is the identity update and satisfies the hypotheses. Every instance of the travelling salesman decision problem, NP-complete by Karp (1972), is then a D-RTSP instance with the same answer. ∎

The proposition locates the phenomenon. Reflexivity is not a hardness property, which is why a deterministic reactive map sits in the same class as a static one, and the limits it does impose are limits on settlement rather than on computation — which is what Theorem 5.5 proves. In the non-deterministic variant the expected cost of a single fixed tour is a sum over exponentially many trajectories of the update rule, so it need not be computable in polynomial time, and the decision problem is not known to lie in NP. The same reversal appears in the Canadian traveller problem of Papadimitriou and Yannakakis (1991), where edge costs are revealed as the traveller arrives, and where the adaptive version is PSPACE-complete. Time-dependent and dynamic vehicle routing, surveyed by Psaraftis (1995), studies the case where the map changes on a schedule rather than in response to the route.

5. Two Undecidability Results

Two limits arise for machines interacting with these systems, and they have different sources. The first fixes a question in advance and reduces the halting problem to it. The second lets the question depend on the probe, and it is the one that does not reduce to any static result.

5.1 Machines That Persist Between Interactions

Definition 5.1 (PITM). A persistent interactive Turing machine is a Turing machine with two additional head actions, O to emit an interaction request and I to receive the resulting outcome, whose configuration, tape, and head position persist across interactions. A PITM is coupled to an RCS: emitting y applies V and T to the system's current state, and the outcome is delivered to the machine's pending I.

This is the persistent Turing machine of Goldin and colleagues (2004), coupled to an environment that transforms according to the outcomes it returns.

Definition 5.2 (Deciding a property). A PITM M decides a property Π on a family F of RCSs when, for every S ∈ F and admissible initial state x0, the coupled run halts, and accepts exactly when Π(S, x0) holds.

5.2 A Fixed Property That No Machine Decides

Theorem 5.3. There is a computable family of D-RCSs and a property TERM, fixed independently of any decider, such that no PITM decides TERM on the family.

Proof. For a Turing machine code e and input w, define the D-RCS Se,w with states the configurations of e on w, interactions Y = {step, read}, and outcomes O = {running, halted}. Let V(x, step) = halted when x is a halting configuration and running otherwise; let T(x, step, running) be the successor configuration and T(x, step, halted) = x; and let read return the last outcome and leave the state fixed. The transformation consults the outcome, so this is a D-RCS in the sense of Definition 2.1, and the map (e, w) ↦ Se,w is computable.

Define TERM(S, x0) to hold when some finite sequence of step interactions yields the outcome halted. By construction TERM(Se,w, x0) holds exactly when e halts on w. A PITM deciding TERM on the family would, given (e, w), construct Se,w and decide halting, which Turing (1936) excludes. ∎

Randomization with bounded error does not help, provided the machine's resources are bounded in a way that keeps its behaviour enumerable.

Theorem 5.4. Take the point-mass ND-RCS versions of the same family, in which every outcome and transition has probability one. Fix a rational 0 < ε < 1/2. No probabilistic PITM with a computable finite runtime bound, and with computable finite supports carrying rational probabilities, decides TERM on the family with success probability at least 1/2 + ε.

Proof. The runtime bound makes the machine's interaction tree finite, and the supports and probabilities are computable rationals, so the acceptance probability on a given instance is computed exactly by enumerating the tree. Under the assumed success rate, acceptance probability exceeds 1/2 on terminating instances and falls below it otherwise. Computing that probability and comparing it with 1/2 would decide halting. ∎

What Theorems 5.3 and 5.4 establish is that the interactive setting inherits classical undecidability rather than escaping it. The property is fixed before any decider is considered, and the strength of the result comes from the halting problem. This is worth stating because the interesting limit is the next one, and it is a different limit.

5.3 A Question That No Interaction Can Settle

Now let the question concern a prediction, and let the system be built to react to it. The obstruction changes character: nothing is undecidable in the classical sense, and instead no answer survives the act of obtaining it.

Theorem 5.5 (No stable verification). Let S be a counter-predictive D-RCS in the sense of Definition 3.3, and let M be a PITM whose run issues at least one prediction interaction and halts. Let y be the last prediction M issues, o the outcome recorded for it, and x′ the state in which the run ends. Then y is accurate at x′ exactly when o = inaccurate. No PITM halts with a recorded verification that agrees with the state it leaves behind.

Proof. Since y is the last prediction issued, no further prediction interaction occurs after it, so the state at the end of the run is x′ = T(x, y, o) for the state x at which y was issued, possibly followed by interactions that Definition 3.3 leaves the prediction's status unchanged under. Lemma 3.4 gives the inversion directly. ∎

Corollary 5.6. For counter-predictive systems there is no protocol that determines whether a prediction holds at the end of the protocol by testing that prediction. Every such test inverts the property it tests.

The difference from Section 5.2 is the source of the difficulty. There the property was fixed and the machine could not compute it. Here the machine computes something exactly, records it correctly, and the record is false of the world the recording produced. No amount of computation repairs this, because computation is not what is missing.

5.4 Which Limit Is Which

The two results are worth keeping apart, because they are often merged.

  • Fixed property, uniform decider excluded. Theorems 5.3 and 5.4 fix TERM in advance and rule out a total decider on the family. The construction is a reduction, the limit is classical, and interaction is the setting rather than the cause.
  • Live property, no stable answer. Theorem 5.5 lets the property depend on the probe. The limit is a fixed-point failure of the kind Lemma 3.4 describes, and it does not follow from the halting problem or from Rice's (1953) theorem, both of which concern properties fixed before the decider is chosen.

Wolpert (2008) proved a related bound for physical inference devices embedded in the universe they measure, and reached the same conclusion by a different route: two such devices cannot be mutually correct, and no device is correct about every question concerning a universe containing it. The reflexive framework isolates the mechanism that forces this in the interactive case, which is the dependence of the transformation on the outcome.

6. What Follows

6.1 For Computation

The interactive setting inherits classical undecidability, which Theorem 5.3 shows, and it also carries a limit of its own, which Theorem 5.5 shows. The second matters for embedded computation, where the algorithm is part of the system it analyses and its queries are events in that system. An algorithm outside the system is not subject to Theorem 5.5, which is the formal reason external audit is worth more than introspection.

6.2 For Modelling and Control

An agent that is part of what it models, and whose actions have consequences that depend on their outcomes, cannot hold a stable complete model of the system it belongs to. Control that assumes perfect prediction is therefore misspecified for this class, and control designed around margins, monitoring, and revision is not. In artificial systems the point is concrete: an agent whose actions change its environment, including the humans and other agents in it, changes the distribution its own forecasts were fitted to, so a forecast validated before deployment is evidence about a system that deployment has altered.

6.3 For Social Systems

Published forecasts change behaviour, which Merton (1948) described as the self-fulfilling prophecy. The reflexive framing sharpens the question into one about fixed points. Where the reaction to a forecast moves the outcome toward it, a stable published forecast can exist, which is the case Grunberg and Modigliani (1954) and Simon (1954) analysed. Where the reaction moves the outcome away, Lemma 3.4 applies and no stable forecast exists. Which case holds is a property of the reaction function, and it is an empirical question about the particular system.

6.4 For Physics and Information

Reflexivity gives the common shape of measurement disturbance, the epistemic obstacles to reversal, and the cost of records, without replacing the physics. The Born rule, the commutator, the second law and Landauer's bound enter as the physics of each case, and what the framework supplies is the structure they share: an outcome that determines how the system it was drawn from changes. That structure carries consequences of its own, proved in Section 3 and applied in Section 5, and they hold across every instance regardless of which physics fills it.

7. Conclusion

Reflexivity is the property that a system's transformation depends on the outcome of the interaction that examined it. Reflexive Computational Systems make that precise in deterministic and probabilistic forms, and three consequences follow from the coupling: prior states can be unrecoverable, what an interaction reveals is a property of the state it produced, and no prediction is stable against a system built to invert it.

Two limits follow for machines. Fixing a property in advance and reducing the halting problem shows that interactive computation inherits classical undecidability, and randomization with bounded error does not evade it. Letting the property depend on the probe shows a limit of a different kind: a machine can compute correctly, record correctly, and still end holding a verification that the world it produced has falsified. The first limit is about what can be computed. The second is about what can be settled, and it is the one that belongs to interaction.

For systems where an observer is embedded and its interactions are transformative, the practical consequence is that the goal of a stable, complete, externally verifiable account has to be replaced. What remains available is exactly what Sections 3 and 5 leave standing: bounded knowledge, revisable models, and predictions that are useful without being fixed points.
