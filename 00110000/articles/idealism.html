Idealism: Simplest Explanation for Consciousness

The question of how consciousness arises out of unconscious matter has been treated as central to philosophy and science for a century. It is a manufactured question. It exists because a premise was adopted first, one that defines the foundation of the world as having no felt side and then demands that feeling be derived from it. Idealism drops the premise, and the question dissolves with it. Nothing is relocated and no residue is left over. The objection usually raised in reply, that a mind could not produce a world as stable and external-seeming as this one, is answered by every dream anyone has ever had.

The Position in Plain Terms

Last night you built a world. It had space you moved through, a horizon, weather. There were other people in it who said things you did not see coming. Objects stayed where you left them. You had a body, a location, and a point of view. While you were inside it you took it for the real one, because it carried every mark of being the real one.

Then you woke, and none of it had been made of matter.

That is the argument in a single observation. A mind generating a complete world that appears external, lawful, and independent of the observer is not a hypothesis in need of defence. It is a nightly occurrence with a laboratory literature behind it. LaBerge et al. (1981) had lucid dreamers agree on an eye-movement signal before sleep and then send it from inside REM sleep, recorded on the polysomnograph, establishing that a person can know they are dreaming and act deliberately while the dream world continues around them. Konkoly et al. (2021) went further and held two-way conversations with sleepers, putting questions in and receiving correct answers in real time. Windt (2015) characterises the dream as an immersive spatiotemporal hallucination, a full world-model with a spatial frame and a self situated inside it, and Metzinger (2003) sets out how the self at the centre of that model is part of the model too.

Now compare what each position is asked to demonstrate. Idealism is asked to show a mind producing an apparently external world. The answer is last night, and every night, in every human being, with electrodes attached. Materialism is asked to show unconscious matter producing an experience. There is no instance to point at, no demonstration, and no account of how the production would go. That asymmetry is the whole case.

The reply usually offered here changes the subject. It asks how experience comes to be stable, lawful, and shared between people, and presents this as a debt idealism has taken on in exchange for the one it cleared. It is neither the hard problem nor a debt. Dreams are already stable and lawful enough to walk around in for hours. And the question of why anything at all is lawful falls on materialism in identical form, since nothing in the concept of matter explains why matter should obey rules. A question both positions face equally is a cost to neither.

Where the Hard Problem Comes From

In the paper that named it, Chalmers (1995) framed the hard problem as the question of why physical processing should be accompanied by experience at all. Read the question closely and the difficulty is carried in its premise. It asks for a route from something specified as having no felt side to something that has one, and the route is impossible because the starting point was defined to exclude the destination. Levine (1983) had already located the same failure inside materialist identity claims, where identifying a mental state with a physical one leaves the felt character unexplained by anything in the physical description.

Both formulations are conditional on starting with matter. The gap is a gap in a derivation, and only one position is obliged to attempt that derivation. The philosophers who examined it most closely drew the conclusion and acted on it: Chalmers, having argued that no physicalist account closes the gap, moved to positions that place experience among the fundamentals, and the panpsychism developed by Goff (2017) makes the same concession explicit by distributing experience through matter rather than deriving it. Idealism takes the shorter route. It declines the premise at the outset instead of repairing the damage afterwards.

The Shortcomings of Materialism and Dualism

Materialist accounts begin with physical processes and then ask consciousness to emerge from them. The emergence has never been exhibited, and the reason is structural: every physical description is a description of behaviour, structure, and relation, and no amount of behaviour, structure, and relation adds up to the felt quality of anything. Dualism keeps consciousness by dividing mind and matter into separate substances, which purchases the felt side at the price of an interaction it cannot describe, since whatever mechanism joins the two realms would belong to a third thing that unifies them.

Both positions pay for the same original move. Matter is placed first, experience is then found to be missing from it, and the rest of the work goes into recovering what the first move discarded. Idealism does not make that move, and so does not pay for it. Empirical investigation of brains and bodies proceeds exactly as before, because that investigation never touched the metaphysics. It correlates one class of observation with another, and every observation in it arrived as an experience.

Idealism as a Unified Explanation

Idealism, in this setting, treats reality as structured experience rather than as two disconnected substances or as experience produced by what is defined as non-experiential. The lineage runs from Berkeley (1710) and Kant (1781) to contemporary versions, whether panpsychist (Goff 2017) or analytic-idealist (Kastrup 2024). The perceived universe is approached as lawlike structure within consciousness, constrained by predictive consistency, shared verification, and physical instantiation.

Idealism begins with the one datum that cannot be doubted, awareness, and adds nothing further. Materialism posits a second category of thing that nobody has encountered outside of experience, and then finds it cannot get back. Dualism posits both categories and a relation between them that it cannot specify. Idealism carries one category, the one every person has direct access to, and Berkeley (1710) made the decisive observation about the alternative: the mind-independent matter said to lie behind experience is itself conceived, described, and argued for entirely within experience, so it is a posit produced by the very faculty it is meant to underwrite.

What Idealism Is Not Required to Explain

Three demands are routinely presented to idealism as though they were the hard problem returning in a new outfit. None of them is.

Why the world is stable. Dreams answer this. A dreaming mind sustains a spatial frame, object permanence, weather, and a functioning body for hours at a stretch, and Windt (2015) documents the structure in detail. Stability is something minds demonstrably do. Materialism, meanwhile, has no account of why matter is stable either. It is assumed.

Why the world is lawful. The laws of physics are compact descriptions of regularities in what has been observed, and every observation used to establish them arrived as an experience: a reading, a mark on a plate, a number on a screen. Those regularities remain exactly what they were once the substrate is understood as experiential, and every equation continues to work. Nothing in physics depended on the metaphysics, which is why physics never had to state one.

Why the world is shared. Agreement between observers is explained the same way on either account, by a common structure that constrains what each observer finds. Materialism calls that structure matter. Idealism calls it the shared substrate whose regularities are what the word physical names, and observers agree because they are perspectives within one such structure rather than separate worlds that happen to correspond. The two accounts posit the same thing and differ only on whether it has a felt side, so every prediction they license is identical — which is why no experiment has ever turned on the difference. What the materialist version adds is the requirement that the common structure be non-experiential, and that requirement buys nothing here and costs the hard problem elsewhere.

The pattern is consistent. Each demand concerns the organisation of experience, each falls on materialism in the same form and with the same force, and none of them asks anyone to produce something felt out of something with no felt side. That last request is what made the hard problem hard, and idealism never issues it.

Conclusion

The hard problem is an artifact. It is produced by the materialist premise, which defines the foundation of the world as non-experiential and then requires that experience be recovered from it. With awareness at the foundation there is nothing to recover, nothing to bridge, and nothing carried forward. The problem does not move. It stops existing, in the way a question stops existing when the false assumption that generated it is withdrawn.

What the objectors have left is the charge that a mind could not sustain a world of this kind. Every person alive refutes it nightly, under laboratory observation when anyone bothers to look (LaBerge et al. 1981; Konkoly et al. 2021; Windt 2015). Mind-made worlds are the one thing in this dispute that has been directly witnessed.

In the Predictive Universe framework, consciousness is the root, prediction is the operation, and physical structure is understood through resource-constrained instantiation. The work ahead lies in that structure: which regularities hold, under what constraints, and what the limits on prediction imply about the world they describe. That is ordinary scientific work, and it was always the only work there was.
