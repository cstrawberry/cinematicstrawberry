Informational Neutrality and the Reevaluation of Dreams in Modern Society

Abstract

This article introduces the concept of informational neutrality and applies it to the study of dreams, advocating for a fundamental reassessment of the value and significance of dream experiences in contemporary society. By challenging the traditional dichotomy between waking and dreaming states, we propose a more holistic approach to consciousness that recognizes the equal validity of all experiential states. This paradigm shift has profound implications for psychology, neuroscience, and cultural practices surrounding sleep and dreaming.

The Idea in Plain Terms

A printed report and a remark overheard on a train are different sources, and most people rank them by where they came from before asking what either one says. Informational neutrality reverses that order. What the content is, what it explains, and what it changes come first; the source is a question about reliability, and reliability is one property among several rather than a gate the content has to pass before it is allowed to matter.

Applied to sleep, the rule cuts against a habit. An idea that arrives at a desk is taken seriously. The same idea arriving at four in the morning gets called just a dream. The argument here is that the hour is not a reason to discard it, and that a night which reorganises a problem has done work whether or not the sleeper was awake for it.

This says nothing about whether dreams are accurate. Dream content is a poor report of the outside world, and nothing here disputes that. What it disputes is the further step, from unreliable report to experience that does not count.

1. Introduction

Human consciousness has long been bifurcated into distinct states, with waking consciousness typically afforded primacy over other states such as dreaming or altered states of consciousness. This hierarchical view has shaped scientific inquiry, cultural attitudes, and individual approaches to personal development.

This paper introduces the principle of informational neutrality and examines its implications for our understanding of dreams. We argue that the current treatment of dreams as secondary to waking experience represents a significant oversight, potentially neglecting half of our lived experience and limiting our understanding of consciousness itself.

2. The Principle of Informational Neutrality

2.1 Defining Informational Neutrality

Informational neutrality posits that the significance of information follows from its content and from the effect it has on a conscious system. Where it came from is a question about reliability, and reliability is one property among several rather than a precondition for counting at all. This principle challenges the primacy often afforded to physical reality in philosophical and practical considerations, suggesting that all sources of information - whether arising from waking or dreaming states - possess equal potential to inform and shape consciousness.

Example: Consider a piece of information received during waking hours versus one encountered in a dream. Traditional perspectives might discount the dream-based information as less reliable or significant. Informational neutrality asserts that both pieces of information should be evaluated based on their interpretative and experiential impact rather than their origin.

3. Current Treatment of Dreams in Society and Science

3.1 Historical Perspectives

Dreams have held varying levels of importance across cultures and throughout history. In ancient civilizations, such as Egypt and Greece, dreams were often regarded as prophetic or divinely inspired, serving as messages from the gods or the subconscious mind. For instance, the Oracle of Delphi was believed to interpret dreams as omens or guidance. In contrast, during the Enlightenment, dreams were often dismissed as irrational or mere byproducts of the unconscious mind. The 20th century saw a resurgence of interest in dreams through psychoanalytic theories, most notably in Freud (1900), who read dreams as disguised wish fulfilment, and in Jung (1959), who read recurring dream imagery as evidence of shared symbolic structure. However, despite these fluctuating perspectives, the dominant view in modern Western society often relegates dreams to a secondary status, primarily focusing on their role in memory consolidation and emotional regulation.

In many contemporary societies, dreams are often dismissed as irrelevant or, at best, curiosities. This attitude is reflected in common phrases like "it's just a dream" or "I'll sleep when i'm dead," which reinforce the notion that dream experiences are less real or significant than waking experiences. Media and popular culture frequently portray dreams as fantastical or nonsensical, further entrenching their marginal status. However, alternative cultural narratives, such as those found in Eastern philosophies, emphasize the interconnectedness and significance of dreams, suggesting a more integrated view of consciousness.

3.2 Contemporary Scientific Approaches

Modern sleep science has made significant strides in understanding the neurophysiology of dreaming. Aserinsky and Kleitman (1953) tied periods of rapid eye movement to frequent and vivid recall, and Nir and Tononi (2010) survey how far the neurophysiology has been mapped since. Siclari et al. (2017) narrowed the correlate further, identifying a posterior cortical zone whose activity predicts whether a sleeper reports an experience, in non-REM sleep as well as in REM. Dreaming turns out to be a property of local cortical activity rather than of a sleep stage. However, the predominant focus remains on how dreams support waking functions, often implicitly relegating them to a supporting role rather than considering them as experiences of equal validity.

4. Applying Informational Neutrality to Dreams

4.1 Dreams as Valid Informational States

From the perspective of informational neutrality, the experiential content of a dream is no less valid or "real" than waking experience. Both states involve the processing of information by the brain, resulting in subjective experiences that shape our understanding of ourselves and the world. The overlap is measurable. Regions that carry perceptual content in waking carry it in sleep, and the posterior zone identified by Siclari et al. (2017) tracks reported experience in both. Dreams are structured, content-bearing states, which is the condition informational neutrality asks them to meet.

4.2 Quantitative Significance

Humans spend roughly a third of life asleep, and about a quarter of that in REM sleep, the phase from which recall is most frequent (Nir and Tononi 2010). Reports also come from non-REM sleep (Siclari et al. 2017), so the experienced share of a night is larger than the REM figure alone suggests. Discounting it writes off a standing fraction of a life on the grounds of when it happened.

4.3 Qualitative Importance

Dreams often provide unique experiences that may be impossible in waking life. They offer novel perspectives, emotional experiences, and creative insights that are not readily available in day-to-day consciousness. For instance, many artists and scientists have reported that dreams have inspired significant creative works and scientific breakthroughs. From the standpoint of informational neutrality, these experiences are equally valuable to waking experiences in shaping our overall consciousness and understanding.

5. Implications of Reevaluating Dreams

5.1 Psychological and Cognitive Sciences

Adopting an informationally neutral stance towards dreams would change which questions get funded. The dominant framings are functional: Revonsuo (2000) treats dreaming as rehearsal for threat, and Wamsley and Stickgold (2011) treat it as the felt side of memory consolidation. Both explain dreaming by what it does for waking. A neutral stance leaves room for a second set of questions, about what the dreaming state contains on its own terms and how that content is organised, which the functional framings do not pose.

5.2 Neuroscience and Consciousness Studies

A more balanced approach to studying waking and dreaming states could provide new insights into the nature of consciousness itself. This could lead to more comprehensive models of consciousness that account for the full spectrum of experiential states. Integrating informational neutrality with existing theories, could enhance our understanding of how different states contribute to the unified experience of consciousness.

5.3 Cultural and Social Impact

Elevating the status of dreams in society could lead to broader cultural changes, potentially influencing work schedules, sleep practices, and social attitudes towards rest and reflection. Societal recognition of the importance of dreaming might encourage practices that support healthy dreaming, such as mindfulness and relaxation techniques.

An informationally neutral approach to dreams could reshape educational practices, potentially leading to the development of "dream literacy" programs that teach individuals how to better remember, interpret, and integrate their dream experiences. Educational curricula might include modules on dream journaling, symbolic analysis, and the application of dream insights to personal and professional goals.

6. Conclusion

Informational neutrality asks for one change: stop ranking an experience by the state it arrived in. Applied to dreams the consequence is immediate. A third of a life stops being preparation for the other two thirds and enters the record as lived experience.

The neuroscience already supports that standing. Dreaming is a content-bearing state with a cortical correlate that tracks reported experience in non-REM sleep as well as REM (Siclari et al. 2017), running on the systems that carry perception in waking (Nir and Tononi 2010).

What changes is the research programme. The dominant framings explain dreaming by what it does for waking, whether rehearsing threat (Revonsuo 2000) or consolidating memory (Wamsley and Stickgold 2011). Treating the dream as an experience in its own right opens the questions those framings never pose: what the state contains, how its content is organised, and what a person gives up by discarding it every morning.
